# Fix Bedrock throughput before leaving London

**Recommendation: Option A. Stay on Bedrock London and fix throughput first. Do not move everything to Anthropic's direct API (Option B).** The law does not force UK-only processing, so a move to the direct API is legally possible. But it would break Avodeck's own rule 5, send health data to the US with no UK or EU inference option, and run on a contract that lists special-category data as "None". It would also swap one quota wall for others (spend caps, tiers, sales-gated limits). The 18-minute wait has a cheaper explanation. A 900-page bundle is about 90 calls, and 90 calls at the app's own cap of 5 per minute is exactly 18 minutes. On Bedrock, each call's 10k output tokens are counted as 100k against the quota. Nobody has yet looked at the account's applied quota, which is probably far below the published 3M-tokens-per-minute default. If A is tried properly and is still too slow, the next step is a per-customer opt-in route (Option C), not a blanket move. This is research, not legal advice, and several items below are marked UNVERIFIED because the notes could not confirm them.

## The 18 minutes is a ceiling Avodeck set, sitting on a quota nobody has checked

Bedrock charges a call against the per-minute token quota twice. At admission it reserves input plus `max_tokens` (here 40k + 16k = **56k**). After the call it settles at input plus output times a burndown factor, and the factor is **10x for Sonnet 5** ([AWS token burndown](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/quotas-token-burndown.html)). Avodeck's call therefore costs about **140k** (40k + 10k x 10), of which 100k is output. The published default for Sonnet 5 on-demand in London is 3,000,000 tokens per minute ([AWS quotas](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/general/latest/gr/bedrock.html); this figure was read by a summariser from a very long page and needs re-checking against the live table). Five calls per minute is about 700k settled, a quarter of that default. Being throttled at that rate suggests the account's applied quota is lower than the default, or that an RPM limit applies. That is an inference from arithmetic, and the account's real values are UNVERIFIED. AWS says defaults can vary with "regional factors, payment history" and approved increases ([Sonnet 4.6 card](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/model-card-anthropic-claude-sonnet-4-6.html)).

The levers in Option A keep 10 pages per call, which is what the founder wants. The first is to read the applied quota in Service Quotas and file an increase request ([AWS quota increases](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/quotas-increase.html)); no turnaround time is published. The second is output size, because output is about 70% of each call's quota cost. Lowering `max_tokens` toward the real 10k trims the admission reservation, and checking whether Sonnet 5's default adaptive thinking is inflating output tokens could remove much more. Both are cheap to test. The third is a separate quota bucket per model. Quotas are set per model, and only the 700,000,000-tokens-per-day cap is shared. Sonnet 4.6 burns only 5x, so a call costs about 90k instead of 140k, and Sonnet 4.6, Opus 4.6 and Opus 5.5 all run in-region in London with their own defaults (3M, 1.5M and 15M tokens per minute, summariser-extracted) ([Opus 5.5 card](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/model-card-anthropic-claude-opus-5-5.html)). Whether those models extract as accurately as Sonnet 5 is not in the notes and needs the nightly eval. Prompt caching helps less than it sounds: cache reads are excluded from final consumption, but the page text that makes up most of the 40k changes on every call, and the notes could not confirm whether cached tokens are excluded at the admission check.

Bedrock has no quick purchase route. Sonnet 5 offers Standard only. Reserved capacity exists for Sonnet 4.6 and Opus 4.6 through the AWS account team, with no published price or term, and Priority, Flex and Provisioned Throughput are not offered for these London models ([Sonnet 5 card](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/model-card-anthropic-claude-sonnet-5.html); [service tiers](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/service-tiers-inference.html)). The Mantle endpoint would avoid the 10x burndown, but it serves Sonnet 5 in-region only in Ireland, Stockholm and some other regions, not London, so using it would break rule 5 anyway ([Sonnet 5 card](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/model-card-anthropic-claude-sonnet-5.html)).

| Scenario (token-rate floor only) | 900 pages (90 calls) | 4,000 pages (about 400 calls) |
|---|---|---|
| Today: self-cap of 5 calls per minute | **18 min** | **about 80 min** |
| Bedrock at published 3M default (140k per call) | at least 4.2 min | at least 19 min |
| Direct API, Start tier (2M input, 400k output per min) | at least 2.3 min | at least 10 min |
| Direct API, Build tier (5M input, 1M output per min) | at least 0.9 min (output-bound) | at least 4 min |

These are my calculations from the notes' figures, not vendor numbers. They ignore how long each call takes to generate 10k tokens, which the notes do not cover, and they ignore spend caps. The direct API's advantage is real: output counts at face value, with no 10x burndown, and cached reads are free of the input limit ([Anthropic rate limits](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/rate-limits)). But a bundle at the published Bedrock default would still take about 4 minutes for 900 pages, so fixing the quota may well meet the target without leaving London.

## What the direct API would and would not fix

The direct API gives faster raw limits but is not a guaranteed capacity upgrade. Sonnet 5 limits are tiered: Start is 1,000 requests, 2M input and 400k output tokens per minute, and Build is 5,000, 5M and 1M ([Anthropic rate limits](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/rate-limits)). Tiers are assigned from usage history, and new organisations may begin in a lower Evaluation tier. Monthly spend caps are **$500 on Start and $1,000 on Build**, and hitting the cap returns a 429 with no retry hint until the 1st. The notes did not retrieve Sonnet 5 pricing, so how fast a few large bundles would reach those caps is unquantified. Priority Tier, the old way to buy guaranteed capacity, is no longer sold and does not support Sonnet 5 or 5.5 ([Anthropic service tiers](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/service-tiers)). Custom limits and Zero Data Retention both go through sales, and how quickly they are granted to a small UK customer is not published.

The residency position is the decisive part. The first-party API's `inference_geo` accepts only `"global"` and `"us"`, and storage at rest is US-only; there is no UK or EU option ([Anthropic data residency](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/data-residency)). US-only inference costs 1.1x. Anthropic's own privacy policy says data goes to the US or other non-EEA countries ([Anthropic privacy policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/privacy)). Anthropic's DPA incorporates the standard contractual clauses (Modules 2 and 3) and the ICO UK Addendum, and Anthropic will supply information for a transfer risk assessment on request ([Anthropic DPA](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/data-processing-addendum)). Avodeck, as the exporter, must carry out that assessment itself. Whether Anthropic is on the UK-US data bridge (the Data Privacy Framework list) is **UNVERIFIED**: the register could not be queried, two secondary reports say Anthropic is absent, one says present, and Anthropic's own documents cite SCCs only ([Compliance Engineering](https://complianceengineering.substack.com/p/openai-and-anthropic-are-not-on-the)). Plan on SCCs and the Addendum, not the bridge.

Bedrock is simpler on privacy. AWS says prompts and outputs are not used for training and are not shared with the model provider, which has no access to the AWS-owned accounts where the model runs ([AWS data protection](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/data-protection.html)). That means Anthropic is not a recipient of Avodeck's data today. Whether Anthropic counts as an AWS sub-processor is UNVERIFIED, and an AWS support letter would settle it. The popular phrase "zero operator access" was not found on an AWS page and should not be used in customer material.

Three further points matter more than the claims themselves. A DPIA is effectively required (large-scale special-category data plus AI) ([ICO DPIA guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/accountability-and-governance/data-protection-impact-assessments-dpias/when-do-we-need-to-do-a-dpia/)). The common-law duty of confidentiality is separate, and whether claimant consent to disclosure covers onward sharing with a US AI provider is an open question in the notes, with no E&W authority found. Finally, Anthropic's Usage Policy (version dated 12 Nov 2026, so not yet in force) treats medical and legal work as high-risk and requires qualified human review and disclosure of AI use ([Anthropic Usage Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup)). Avodeck's design, where a named expert reviews everything and the AI writes no opinion, probably fits, but that is my reading, so ask Anthropic to confirm in writing.

## What changing rule 5 would involve

Rule 5 is Avodeck's own policy, not law, and the notes found no published demand for UK-only processing from NHS Resolution, the defence unions or named firms. The nearest source is NHS England's offshoring guidance, which sets UK-only hosting as the default for NHS and social care organisations but allows EEA, adequate countries or the US under an IDTA with senior approval ([NHS England](https://digital.nhs.uk/data-and-information/looking-after-information/data-security-and-information-governance/nhs-and-social-care-data-off-shoring-and-the-use-of-public-cloud-services)). It binds NHS bodies, not Avodeck directly, but buyers may pass equivalent terms down. Whether any named firm does so is UNVERIFIED, since procurement questionnaires are private. The customer-side risk is therefore commercial, not legal, and it falls hardest on defendant-side and indemnifier-linked buyers. Sortica, a UK competitor, markets London storage and an EU-only endpoint, so disclosure of AI location is already a selling point ([Sortica](https://sortica.ai/dataprotection)).

Changing the rule is a package of work. It needs a new decision record superseding rule 5 and ADR 0021, with foundation-owner approval because AGENTS.md is foundation-owned and the guard tests encode the rule. Under Article 28(2) the customer (expert or solicitor) must authorise any new sub-processor, and Avodeck must tell controllers of the change and let them object ([UK GDPR Art 28](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/28)). Customer DPAs and sub-processor lists need updating, and Anthropic's own sub-processor objection window is 15 days, so Avodeck's notice terms should not be shorter. A transfer risk assessment under the "not materially lower" test and an updated DPIA are needed, as is a written fix to the "None" row. Website claims, security pages and questionnaire answers that say data stays in the UK must change. The wording of existing claims also needs care: the API and worker already run in Amsterdam (holding no data at rest), so "UK only" in marketing should already be worded precisely.

## Options compared

Option A costs a few days of engineering and one AWS request, and changes nothing legal. Its risk is that the applied quota or AWS approval time is worse than hoped, which is why the checks below come first. Option B gives the highest raw limits, but only after Anthropic assigns a tier, grants ZDR and lifts spend caps, none of which is guaranteed; it moves every customer's records to the US with the "None" contract wording unresolved. Option C, routing only customers who opt in, is the right shape if A falls short, because the choice then belongs to the controller, who can document necessity and proportionality for the transfer. It carries the cost of two code paths, two risk assessments, split marketing claims and tests that prove the routing cannot misfire. One intermediate step was not in the original list: Bedrock's `eu.` profile roughly doubles the published cross-region quota for Sonnet 5 (6M versus 3M) and keeps the AWS contract and no new sub-processor. It sends data to EU regions (Frankfurt, Stockholm and others), which breaks rule 5 but not, on the notes' reading, UK GDPR ([Sonnet 4.6 card](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/model-card-anthropic-claude-sonnet-4-6.html)). The notes list the `eu.` regions for Sonnet 4.6, Opus 4.6 and Opus 5.5, and the 6M Sonnet 5 figure is summariser-extracted. Its availability for Sonnet 5 is UNVERIFIED, and it would still need a decision record and customer notice.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "which vendor?" to "which wall are we hitting?". Today's 18 minutes is the product of Avodeck's own 5-calls-a-minute ceiling on a Bedrock quota whose real size is unknown, and of a 10x output burndown that a direct API would not apply. Those are fixable in London in days. The legal analysis supports the founder's instinct that the US route is possible, but it also shows that what is hard about it is the paperwork around Avodeck's own promise: a customer-by-customer authorisation chain, a risk assessment, a contract that currently says "None" for special categories, and a competitor (Sortica) that publicises the opposite choice.

The most useful new fact is the shape of any later compromise. If Option A reaches only part of the way, the defensible next step is to let controllers opt in to a faster route, with the decision recorded, and not to rewrite every customer's data flow. Two things would change this advice: an applied Bedrock quota that AWS refuses to raise, or a customer base that treats UK-only processing as unimportant. The notes have no evidence for either yet.
